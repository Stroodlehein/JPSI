JPMI Research Note · May 2026 · Global Pricing

Why Japan Physical Silver Trades Above Global Benchmarks

Japan’s physical silver premium is one of the clearest examples of regional physical pricing divergence. It shows why global pricing is not always the same as local pricing — especially when currency pressure, inventory friction, and demand for hard assets collide.

The core observation

JPMI tracks Japan’s physical silver market by comparing local retail and buyback pricing with global benchmarks such as COMEX equivalents. The recurring pattern is simple: Japanese physical silver can trade far above the global paper price. This premium is not just a curiosity. It is a useful signal of how real-world physical markets can behave differently from futures-driven benchmarks.

The key lesson is that “spot price” does not always mean “available local physical price.” COMEX may provide the global reference point, but retail buyers in Japan face a different market: a smaller supply chain, different dealer structure, import costs, currency risk, and local demand for physical metal.

1. Weak yen pressure

A weaker yen directly raises the local cost of imported bullion. Even when global silver prices are stable in dollar terms, Japanese buyers may still experience rising local prices if the yen weakens. This matters because Japan imports much of its investment-grade bullion exposure. Currency weakness also changes investor psychology. When purchasing power declines, more buyers begin to look at hard assets as a store of value.

2. Dealer and delivery friction

The premium is not explained by currency alone. Local availability also matters. If dealers face inventory stress, shipping delays, or wholesale supply pressure, the price of immediately available physical silver can rise. In a smaller retail market, even modest demand can have a larger effect on available supply. Buyers are not only paying for silver content. They are paying for access, delivery, certainty, and local availability.

Global pricing is not always local pricing.

3. Paper vs physical structure

COMEX is a global benchmark, but it is primarily a paper market. Most participants are trading exposure, not taking physical bars into their own possession. Local retail markets are different. They involve finished products, dealer margins, logistics, taxes, shipping, inventory constraints, and buyer preference. During calm periods, these differences may appear small. During stress periods, the gap can become highly visible.

4. Why arbitrage may not close the gap quickly

In theory, if silver is cheaper overseas, traders should buy abroad and sell into Japan until the premium disappears. In practice, physical arbitrage is slower and messier than spreadsheet math. Shipping, insurance, customs, product form, dealer trust, payment rails, resale liquidity, and timing all matter. A retail coin premium is not the same thing as a clean institutional arbitrage spread. That is why regional premiums can persist longer than many traders expect.

5. The global implication

Japan may be one of the clearest examples, but it may not be the only one. Shanghai, Germany, India, and other physical markets have also drawn attention for elevated premiums or local supply stress. If multiple regional markets begin pricing physical silver above global benchmarks at the same time, the signal becomes larger than Japan. It becomes evidence of regional physical pricing divergence.

Conclusion

The Japan silver premium should not be viewed only as a local oddity. It is a window into how physical markets actually behave when global benchmarks meet local demand, currency weakness, and real-world supply chains. JPMI exists to track this gap. Not to predict the future, but to observe when paper pricing and physical pricing begin telling different stories.

JPMI Research Note · June 2026 · Market Structure

Regional Physical Pricing Divergence: Japan, Shanghai, Germany, and India

Physical silver is often quoted as a single global market, but local pricing can diverge sharply when demand, inventory, taxes, currency pressure, and distribution constraints differ by region.

A global price, but not always a global reality

Physical silver is often described as a global market. Prices are quoted in U.S. dollars, benchmarked against COMEX futures, and referenced by investors around the world. Yet anyone who has attempted to buy physical silver in different countries quickly discovers that the global market is not as uniform as it appears.

In practice, physical silver frequently trades at different prices depending on location, local demand, available inventory, import costs, taxes, and distribution networks. During periods of market stress, these differences can become substantial. What appears to be a single global silver market can instead behave as a collection of regional physical markets connected by a common benchmark but influenced by local conditions.

Japan: local demand and physical access

Japan provides a useful example. JPMI tracks physical silver pricing across refiners, dealers, and street-level retail transactions. At various points, physical silver available through Japanese retail channels has traded significantly above COMEX-derived pricing. This divergence is not necessarily caused by a shortage of silver itself. More often, it reflects local supply and demand conditions, retail investor activity, and the costs associated with moving physical metal through the distribution chain.

Shanghai: another physical pricing center

Shanghai represents another important physical pricing center. China is one of the world's largest consumers and importers of precious metals. When domestic demand rises, physical premiums within the Chinese market can increase independently of developments in North America or Europe. The Shanghai market has at times traded at meaningful premiums to international benchmarks, creating regional pricing signals that may not be immediately visible in futures markets.

A single silver benchmark does not guarantee a single physical market.

India and Germany: different drivers, same question

India offers a different but equally important case study. Silver ownership has deep cultural and economic roots throughout the country. Demand can be influenced by seasonal purchasing patterns, agricultural income, currency movements, and local investment sentiment. Physical premiums in India often respond to factors that have little connection to trading activity on COMEX. As a result, local pricing may strengthen even during periods when international benchmark prices appear stable.

Germany provides a European perspective on physical market behavior. European investors have historically maintained strong interest in physical precious metals as a form of savings and wealth preservation. Tax policies, regulatory changes, and shifts in investor demand can all influence physical pricing. During periods of economic uncertainty, demand for coins and bars may increase rapidly, creating temporary dislocations between physical products and futures-based benchmarks.

When regional premiums become a larger signal

The key observation is that these regional markets do not always move in lockstep. A premium developing in Japan may be driven by entirely different factors than a premium emerging in Shanghai or India. Yet when multiple regions begin exhibiting elevated physical pricing at the same time, the signal becomes more significant.

If physical premiums appear simultaneously across several major markets, it may suggest that demand for immediate ownership of silver is strengthening globally. Futures markets continue to establish the benchmark price, but physical buyers may be assigning greater value to actual possession and available inventory. The result is a widening gap between paper benchmarks and physical market realities.

Conclusion

For investors, these divergences provide additional information that is often overlooked. Futures prices alone may not fully capture conditions within physical supply chains. Monitoring regional premiums can offer insight into where demand is strongest, how inventory is moving, and whether physical markets are becoming tighter than benchmark prices imply.

The global silver market may share a common reference price, but it does not always share a common reality. Understanding regional physical pricing divergence may become increasingly important as investors seek to understand the relationship between paper benchmarks and the markets where actual metal changes hands.

Weak Yen, Hard Assets, and the Japan Silver Premium

Japan’s silver market provides a useful test of a familiar hard-asset argument: when a currency weakens, local investors may place greater value on physical assets. The yen clearly affects the domestic price of silver, but JPMI data shows that currency conversion alone cannot explain the premium paid for physical metal.

The yen raises the starting price

Silver is internationally priced in U.S. dollars. For a Japanese buyer, the yen price therefore depends on two moving variables: the global silver price and USD/JPY.

A weaker yen raises the domestic cost of silver even if its dollar price remains unchanged. At the JPMI observation on August 10, COMEX silver was approximately $64.10 per ounce, while USD/JPY stood at ¥158.34. Converted into Japanese terms, COMEX was worth approximately ¥326.30 per gram.

This currency-adjusted price is the correct starting point for analysing Japan. Comparing a Japanese coin price directly with the dollar COMEX quote would mix currency depreciation with the actual physical premium. But once COMEX has already been converted into yen, any remaining gap requires another explanation.

The premium remaining after conversion

On August 10, JPMI recorded four distinct market layers: COMEX equivalent at ¥326.30/g, the dealer and pawn composite at ¥329.00/g, the refinery composite at ¥345.77/g, and Mercari MSPI-B at ¥414.33/g.

The overall JPMI-AG benchmark stood at ¥363.03/g, approximately 11.3% above the currency-adjusted COMEX price. At street level, the three qualifying one-ounce Mercari transactions averaged ¥12,900 per coin, placing MSPI-B approximately 27% above COMEX.

That distinction matters. Yen weakness helps explain why the entire Japanese price structure is high in nominal yen, but it does not explain why a physical coin changes hands at ¥414/g when the same quantity of silver is represented by COMEX at ¥326/g. The additional premium belongs to the physical market.

Currency determines the local benchmark. Market structure determines the premium above it.

Physical ownership carries local costs

A one-ounce bullion coin on Mercari is not interchangeable with a futures quotation. It is an immediately available, recognisable physical product already located inside Japan.

Its price incorporates fabrication, importation, delivery, platform fees, seller expectations and limited local availability. Mercari itself deducts a 10% seller commission, meaning the buyer-paid price also differs materially from the amount ultimately received by the seller.

Japan’s retail bullion market is relatively small. A modest change in available listings or buyer activity can therefore move the observed street price more sharply than it would in a deeper market. Physical arbitrage is also imperfect: overseas shipping, insurance, customs procedures, payment costs, product acceptance and resale liquidity prevent every apparent price difference from being closed immediately.

Does the premium prove hard-asset demand?

The hard-asset explanation is plausible, but it should not be overstated. Years of yen weakness, rising import costs and declining purchasing power may encourage some Japanese savers to view precious metals as protection against monetary depreciation. Physical ownership offers something that a paper contract does not: direct possession of an asset outside the banking system.

However, JPMI observes prices and completed transactions, not the private motivation of every buyer. A high Mercari price cannot, by itself, prove that buyers are consciously hedging the yen.

Transaction activity must also be considered. The August 10 MSPI-B reading was based on only three qualifying sales. Thin liquidity can produce volatile daily averages, particularly when the available coins differ in condition, mint and buyer appeal. A high premium accompanied by strong transaction volume would indicate something different from a high premium based on only a handful of sales.

This is why JPMI increasingly treats price and liquidity as connected signals. Falling street prices combined with fewer qualifying sales may indicate cooling retail demand, while persistent premiums supported by active turnover would suggest firmer physical-market strength.

What the current structure tells us

The latest data does not show one uniform Japan silver price. Instead, it shows a layered market. Dealer bids are currently close to the COMEX equivalent. Refinery prices sit moderately higher. Mercari transactions remain substantially higher because they represent finished bullion coins changing hands between retail participants.

The yen affects every layer, but each layer responds differently to availability, transaction costs and market depth.

Conclusion

Weakness in the yen raises the domestic foundation beneath Japanese silver prices. It may also strengthen the appeal of physical assets as stores of purchasing power. But it does not independently create the Japan silver premium.

That premium emerges where currency pressure meets physical availability, retail behaviour, distribution friction and liquidity. Tracking those forces together, not merely converting COMEX into yen, is necessary to understand what Japanese buyers are actually paying for physical silver.
